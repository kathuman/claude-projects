About Heat Pump Studio

Heat Pump Studio v1.4.0

Heat Pump Studio designs a heat pump the way an engineer does: from the heat the building needs, through the heat source and the heating circuit, to the refrigerant cycle and the components that make it work. It covers homes and larger commercial and industrial machines.

New to heat pumps? The Learn page explains what they are and walks through the design equations with a worked example, for readers from about 15.

The toy heat pump lets you build one from parts and run it between two buckets of water, with a shopping list for building a real one.

How it works

The heat load is the building's heat-loss coefficient times the difference between indoor and design outdoor temperature, plus an allowance for hot water. The source and the heating circuit set the evaporating and condensing temperatures, through the temperature drop of the source and the approach of each heat exchanger. The single-stage cycle follows the refrigerant through compression (with the compressor's isentropic efficiency), condensation, expansion and evaporation; the heat exchangers are split into zones (desuperheating, condensing, subcooling; evaporating, superheating), each sized with its own temperature difference, which finds pinch problems inside them.

Refrigerant properties

Properties come from CoolProp, the open-source property library: tables of saturated states and of superheated vapour and subcooled liquid, generated once and interpolated in the browser. The tests compare them with CoolProp at hundreds of random states and with complete CoolProp cycles; the COP agrees within 0.5 %.

The year round

The seasonal performance follows the bin method of EN 14825, with real climates: ten years (2015–2024) of hourly temperatures for each location, from the Open-Meteo historical weather archive (ERA5 reanalysis, CC BY 4.0). For every degree of outdoor temperature the building's load falls linearly to the heating limit; the inverter heat pump follows it, with weather-compensated flow temperatures, defrost losses for air sources and on/off cycling below its minimum speed, and an electric heater covers the rest. That gives the SCOP, the balance point, the running costs and the CO₂ against a gas boiler.

Limits

Pressure drops are neglected. Heat-transfer coefficients, volumetric efficiencies, the charge estimate and the borehole guide values are typical figures for a first design. The cycle is single stage and subcritical, so CO₂ (R744), which runs above its critical point, is not included yet. Check every design against manufacturers' data and the standards that apply where it will be built.

About the author

Heat Pump Studio was designed and built by Daniel Alberto Sepulveda Estay, PhD, founder of Estay Dynamics. He is a mechanical engineer by training: a Civil Mechanical Engineer (BSc) from Universidad Técnica Federico Santa María in Valparaíso, Chile, where he focused on thermodynamics, turbomachines, heat transfer and fluid dynamics, and a Dipl.-Ing. (BA) in Mechanical Engineering from the Baden-Württemberg Cooperative State University (DHBW) in Mosbach, Germany, with a focus on modelling and simulation (CFD and finite elements) and an industry thesis with WIKA on mechanical pressure gauges for extreme conditions. This studio stands on that core: the refrigerant cycle, the heat exchangers and the compressor are classic mechanical engineering.

He went on to operations and supply chains, with an MSc in Industrial Engineering from the Pontificia Universidad Católica de Chile, an SM in Management Science from MIT and a PhD in Operations Management from the Technical University of Denmark (DTU), including a year as a visiting researcher at the MIT Center for Transportation & Logistics. He has worked in mining (BHP), beverage distribution (Coca-Cola), healthcare (the Capital Region of Denmark and Rigshospitalet) and pharmaceutical manufacturing, where he is now Global Supply Chain Design Lead at Novo Nordisk in Copenhagen. He has taught system dynamics at MIT and DTU, modelled how ventilation affects the airborne spread of COVID-19, serves as an external examiner for engineering degree projects at Danish universities, and is a council member of the System Dynamics Society.
