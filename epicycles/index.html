The circles

Each circle is one term r cos(k t + φ). It starts at angle φ and turns k times while the pen goes round once, so term k spins k times as fast as term 1.

How it works

  1. One closed line. The picture is reduced to pixels on its outline (an Otsu threshold) or on its edges (a Sobel filter). These are thinned on a grid and joined into one cycle: nearest neighbour first, then 2-opt, which reverses any stretch whose reversal makes the line shorter, so crossings go away. Separate shapes get joined by straight jumps, because a single line has to visit them all.
  2. Even steps. The cycle is resampled to 1,024 points evenly spaced along its length. A parameter t runs from 0 to 2π once round.
  3. One series per side. Each coordinate is expanded separately: x(t) = a₀ + Σ rk cos(k t + φk), and the same for y(t). Each term is one circle. Chained end to end above the picture, the circles' tip sets x; chained down the left, it sets y. The guide lines from the two tips meet at the pen.
  4. One, two, three … With one circle per side the pen draws an ellipse, the best single-frequency fit. Each extra circle adds detail at a higher speed. The error falls toward zero as the count rises.

Taylor or Fourier?

These turning circles are a Fourier series. A Taylor series in time, c₀ + c₁t + c₂t² + …, never comes back to where it started, so it cannot draw a closed loop. Still, the two ideas are closely related. Write z = eit, a point going round the unit circle once per cycle. Then x(t) = Re(A₀ + A₁z + A₂z² + A₃z³ + …) with Ak = rkeiφk. That is a Taylor (power) series in z. Each power zk is a point going round k times as fast, so the term's place in the expansion sets its circle's speed, and its coefficient sets the circle's size and starting angle.
