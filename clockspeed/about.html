About the app

Clockspeed Balancing

Commercial strategy changes in months, the product supply chain in a year, the network in years. Clockspeed Balancing is a management flight simulator for that conflict: you steer a company for 20 years, one quarter at a time, and see how every gap between the three wears down the slow, inertial parts of the company that no one decides on directly.

Open the simulator →

What it does

  • Three levels of decisionsCommercial strategy (price, service promise, promotions, launches, markets), the product supply chain (inventory cover, flexible capacity, dual sourcing) and the network (building, closing and automating capacity by region), each taking effect at its own speed.
  • The inertial companyOperational capability, trust between functions, supplier relationships, customer perception and expectations, product-range complexity, sunk assets and cash, all moving slowly and all affected by misfit.
  • WeatherSix scenarios, from calm skies to an innovation wave, Eastern growth, a promise war, a storm of disruption and tariffs, and all of them at once.
  • A cockpitGauges, fit bars for each level, advisories in plain words, a radar of decisions still in flight, the regions, and 20-year history charts. Undo, autosave, flight files, and a comparison at landing with holding the starting course.

How it is built

The model is a system dynamics model in the tradition of Sterman's management flight simulators: two product segments in three regions, market shares driven by attractiveness, customers who notice failures faster than recoveries and whose expectations ratchet up with every promise, capacity built through a 30-month pipeline, and a capability that firefighting erodes. Its tests check a steady state that holds for 20 years, exact accounting, the three clock speeds (about 4, 10 and 33 months), and the conflicts it exists to show: the promise ratchet, the cost of an unsupported commercial push, the value of pacing, firefighting, complexity creep and resilience. Everything runs in your browser. The name follows Charles Fine's Clockspeed (1998).
