How this works — and how it's validated

The solver is a D3Q19 lattice-Boltzmann method: each grid node carries 19 populations that collide and stream, which reproduces the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations. It runs in a background thread (src/sim-worker.js) so the view stays smooth. The numerics are chosen for accuracy, not looks: curved walls by interpolated bounce-back (every lattice link knows exactly where it crosses the sphere or the tube wall), TRT collision where the grid Reynolds number is low and regularised collision where it is high, a velocity inlet and a pressure outlet by non-equilibrium extrapolation — so mass is conserved without corrections and there are no hidden velocity or density clamps.

Your case, in real units. Pick a fluid, a tube and a sphere and a speed: the app computes the Reynolds number Re = U·d/ν and maps it onto the grid at a low Mach number. Forces come out in newtons, pressure in pascals, time in seconds. If a case needs more resolution than the grid has (air at everyday speeds is Re in the thousands), it runs the highest Reynolds number the grid can resolve and says so, rather than showing a flow it can't compute.

Measurements. Drag and lift on the sphere by momentum exchange on the boundary links; the drag and lift coefficients C = F / (½ρU²·πd²/4) with U the centre-line (pipe) or sphere (moving) speed; the Strouhal number St = f·d/U from the dominant frequency of the lift once it oscillates; the pressure drop across the sphere; and the length of the recirculation bubble behind it on the axis. The regime shown is measured (is there a bubble? does the lift oscillate? has the drag settled?), not looked up from a table — confinement in a tube shifts the thresholds known for a sphere in open flow.

Validation (tests/lbm.test.js, run on every change): the pipe-flow velocity profile and pressure gradient against Hagen–Poiseuille (0.5% and 3%), mass conservation, the Stokes drag on a sphere moving along a tube against the Haberman–Sayre wall-corrected value (3%, with the sphere only six cells across), a momentum balance between pressure drop and the forces on the sphere and wall, and the appearance of the recirculation bubble with Reynolds number.

GPU. When the browser supports WebGPU, the same scheme runs on the graphics card (src/lbm-gpu.js), on grids up to 96 cells across the tube — the sphere is then 20–30 cells across, enough to resolve vortex shedding. tests/gpu.test.js runs both solvers on the same cases and requires the same answer (velocity within 2·10⁻⁵, drag within 0.01%). A long GPU benchmark of a sphere in nearly unbounded flow reproduces Johnson & Patel's measured wake length at Re 100 within 3%. Without WebGPU, the CPU solver (6–14 cells across the sphere) still handles steady flows.
