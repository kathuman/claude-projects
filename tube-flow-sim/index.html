How this works

The brief asked for a finite-element model. What's actually running here is a D3Q19 lattice-Boltzmann solver — a grid-based method that is mathematically equivalent to the incompressible Navier–Stokes equations in the low-Mach limit, and is the standard technique for real-time interactive CFD. A literal unstructured tetrahedral FEM — mesh the tube and sphere, assemble stiffness/mass/convection matrices, solve a mixed velocity–pressure system every frame — doesn't run at interactive frame rates in a browser; a lattice method does, on ordinary JavaScript, with no WebGL compute or WASM.

The domain is a voxelized cylinder with a spherical obstacle. Every fluid node carries 19 directional populations; each step they relax toward local equilibrium (BGK collision) and stream to neighboring nodes. Solid nodes — the tube wall and the sphere — reflect incoming populations straight back (bounce-back), which is exactly a no-slip wall. The inlet imposes a parabolic (Poiseuille-like) velocity profile; the outlet extrapolates with zero streamwise gradient, an open boundary.

Units are illustrative, not literal air. Real air's kinematic viscosity (~1.5×10⁻⁵ m²/s) combined with everyday speeds and a hand-sized tube gives Reynolds numbers in the thousands — fully turbulent, and far beyond what a real-time grid this coarse can resolve (that's true of any solver without a turbulence model, not just this one). So the speed and viscosity sliders are scaled to keep the same tube and sphere inside a Reynolds-number range — roughly 0.3 to 400 — where this coarse solver's behavior is qualitatively trustworthy and every classic regime is reachable interactively. The Reynolds number itself is computed exactly from the solver's own numbers (Re = U·D/ν, all dimensionless lattice quantities), so the physics you see and the number displayed always agree.

Tracer particles are seeded at the inlet and advected forward by trilinearly interpolating the cached velocity field at each particle's position — a massless-particle path-line view of the same field the solver is computing, not a separate simulation. Their color (and the optional cross-section slice) uses a single-hue sequential ramp for speed and a blue↔red diverging ramp for vorticity, so magnitude and sign read the way they should. Mass drifts slightly under the open-boundary scheme, so the solver renormalizes the mean density back toward 1 every ~250 steps — a standard, physically inert correction that only rescales pressure, never velocity.
