What is a real option in supply chain network design?

A classic network study runs a big optimisation, picks the least-cost design for the expected forecast, and commits. That treats the forecast as if it were certain. It never is — demand grows, stalls or collapses, input costs swing, a region opens or closes.

A real option is the right, but not the obligation, to take a future action — expand a site, defer a build, exit a lease, switch a source — for a price agreed today. Designing the network so those rights exist costs money now (a premium: land you don't yet use, modular kit, a second qualified supplier, a shorter lease). In exchange you get to wait for uncertainty to resolve and then act only in the states of the world where acting pays.

The core result — the same one that makes financial options valuable — is that this flexibility is worth more when the future is more uncertain. A rigid least-cost design captures the full downside and a capped upside; a flexible design truncates the downside and keeps the upside. This page lets you price that difference and watch it move.

Deferral option

Wait & see

Use a 3PL or short lease now; hold site permits. Build owned capacity only once demand is confirmed.

Growth option

Stage & expand

Build a small facility on a big footprint, with modular racking and utilities sized for phase 2. Add capacity in steps.

Exit / switch option

Contract & switch

Lease not buy, negotiate break clauses, dual-source, keep multi-product lines. Shed or redirect capacity if demand falls.

The demand lattice & the decision policy

Demand evolves as a recombining binomial tree: each year it multiplies by u (up) or d (down). Node colour is the demand level; a badge marks where the chosen strategy exercises its option. Hover any node for its numbers.

Payoff shape — NPV vs how demand actually turns out

Every path through the lattice is one dot: horizontal is that path's realised demand growth, vertical is the strategy's NPV on that path. Commit now is a straight line with a capped top (you can't serve above capacity) and a full downside. The flexible strategies bend — downside truncated, upside kept. That convex kink is the option.

Why uncertainty makes flexibility valuable

Value of each option = its expected NPV minus Commit now's. Sweeping demand volatility shows the signature real-options result: as the forecast becomes less trustworthy, the option value climbs — flexibility is worth most exactly when you know least. The dashed line is your current setting.

How the model works

Demand

Recombining binomial tree. u = e^σ, d = 1/u. The up-probability p is set so expected growth equals your drift g: p = (e^g − d)/(u − d).

Yearly cash flow

margin·min(D,K) − stockout·max(D−K,0) − opcost·K. Overbuilding is punished by opcost·K; undersupply by lost margin plus a stockout penalty.

Strategies

Commit now: build the full footprint at year 0. Wait & see: build nothing until a node where building beats waiting; later builds cost more (escalation). Stage & expand: build the small footprint now, then add capacity step by step where it pays — each step carries the staging premium.

Valuation

Backward induction over the lattice. Each strategy's optimal policy is solved, then applied forward along every path. NPV discounts expected cash flow at the WACC; a terminal node is capitalised as a level perpetuity.

Deliberate simplifications

One facility, one product. Expected cash flows are discounted at a fixed WACC rather than by formal risk-neutral valuation. The staging premium lumps every diseconomy of building twice into one number. Competition, correlated risks and managerial constraints are out of scope.

Read it as

A teaching model, not a project appraisal. The point is the direction and rough size of the flexibility premium, and which assumptions it hinges on.
