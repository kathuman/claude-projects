Cube Studio

A 3×3 cube in the browser. Scramble it from a truly random position, then watch a short solution play out move by move, in 3D or on a flat net of the six faces. Everything runs on your device.

Reading the moves

Each letter names a face as you hold the cube: Up, Down, Right, Left, Front, Back. A letter alone turns that face a quarter turn clockwise, as you look at that face; with ′ (written ') it turns anticlockwise, and with 2 a half turn. So R U R' U' is right face clockwise, top clockwise, right face back, top back.

The colours follow the usual scheme: white opposite yellow, green opposite blue, red opposite orange. The app holds the cube with white up and green in front.

Turning it yourself

  • Drag a sticker to turn its layer the way you drag it (a middle row or column turns a slice). Drag the background to look around.
  • Keys: U R F D L B turn a face, M E S a slice, X Y Z the whole cube; with Shift they turn the other way. Ctrl+Z undoes, Ctrl+Y (or Ctrl+Shift+Z) redoes.
  • Buttons for every face, slice and rotation: clockwise, anticlockwise and twice.
  • Type moves and press Play to play them, or Set up to start from a solved cube with them already made. Spaces and brackets are optional: (R U R' U') and RUR'U' both work. Wide turns are written Rw or r.
  • Undo and redo: the move list is the history. A move made after stepping back replaces what came after.
  • Copy link gives an address that opens this cube with this move list (?setup=…&alg=…).

A cube turned as a whole (x, y, z) can still be solved: the solution starts by turning it back to the standard hold (white up, green in front).

Your own cube

Press Enter your own cube, then either paint it or scan it.

  • Paint: hold your cube with the white centre up and the green centre facing you, pick a colour, and click the stickers on the net. Each face is drawn as you look straight at it, and the cube on the stage follows as you paint.
  • Scan: the camera reads the faces one at a time, with the app saying how to hold the cube each time.

The scan copes with uneven light. The page compares colours the way people see them (CIELAB), learns how each face was lit, and takes exactly nine stickers of each colour. The picture never leaves your device: nine colours are read from it and the frame is dropped.

Before your cube is used, it is checked. A cube taken apart and put back together at random can be solved only one time in twelve, so a real cube passes four tests:

  • nine stickers of each colour;
  • every piece one that exists, and only once;
  • the corners' twists add up, and so do the edges' flips;
  • the pieces' swaps pair up between corners and edges.

Each failure is explained, with the stickers involved marked. A twisted corner or a flipped edge can't be fixed by turning: take it out and put it back the right way. After a scan, a misreading always fails these checks, so it is never silently solved as the wrong cube. When a reading is impossible, the least certain stickers are marked to look at first.

The robot

The robot is a turntable under the cube and two arms, one on each side, for a LEGO MINDSTORMS Robot Inventor hub. Each part has one motor, and together they can do three things:

StepWhat happensIn cube notation
spinArms open, the turntable turns the whole cube.y, y', y2
turnBoth arms grip the upper two layers, the turntable turns the bottom layer.D, D', D2
tip L / tip RThe left arm tips the cube over to the right, or the right arm to the left.z, z'

So the robot only ever turns the bottom face. For an R, the left arm first rolls the cube so the right face is at the bottom, then the turntable turns it. A cube can sit in the robot 24 ways, so the planner works through all of them: for each move of the solution, it finds the quickest way to bring that face down, given the moves still to come. It can also choose how you put the cube in, and then tells you which colour goes on top and which faces you. A typical solve is about 50 robot steps, roughly 80 seconds with the planner's default timings (your build's real speed will differ).

The drawing on the stage is a simple generic robot, so you can follow the plan. Your own build can look quite different, as long as it moves the same way.

Solving a cube with the robot, step by step

  1. Get your cube into the app. Paint it or scan it with Enter your own cube, or scramble one here to try things out.
  2. Choose how it goes in. Under Put in, the quickest way lets the planner pick which colour goes on top. As shown keeps the cube the way the stage shows it, with the front face towards you.
  3. Press Plan the robot's solve. The panel tells you how to put the cube in, for example "the yellow centre on top and the red centre facing you". The hub is at the back, so "facing you" means the side away from the hub. The panel also shows the number of steps and an estimated time. The move list then holds the robot's steps: press play to watch them, or step through them one at a time.
  4. Check Hub settings against your build (see below). This matters most before the first run.
  5. View program, then Download it as cube_robot.py, or Copy it.
  6. Run it on the hub. The hub needs Pybricks firmware. Paste the program into Pybricks Code and press run, or from a PC run python -m pybricksdev run ble -n <hub name> cube_robot.py.
  7. Start it. Put the cube in as the panel said, with both arms open. The hub shows GO and a yellow light: press its right button. The light turns blue and the screen counts down the steps left. At the end it shows OK with a green light. The centre button stops the robot at any moment.

Each plan belongs to one position. If the cube is turned by hand, or a step slips, plan again from the cube as it is now.

What the program does on the hub

The program is short, and you can read it in View program. It starts with the settings for your build, then the plan as a list of codes, then three moves built from the motors:

  • a spin turns the turntable a quarter or half turn with both arms open;
  • a turn closes both arms to grip, turns the turntable, then opens the arms. The turntable goes a few degrees past and comes back, so the layer settles square;
  • a tip swings one arm to its tip angle, which pushes the cube over, then back to open.

The program takes the motors' positions at start-up as zero. So the arms must be open when it starts, and every arm angle is measured from there.

Hub settings

SettingWhat it meansDefault
Turntable, left arm, right arm portWhere each motor is plugged in (A to F). Each motor needs its own port.A, B, C
Gear ratioHow many degrees the motor turns for one degree of the turntable. A 12-tooth gear driving a 36-tooth gear gives 3. Set it to 1 if the motor drives the turntable directly.3
Arm grip °How far an arm swings from open to hold the upper two layers firmly.90
Arm tip °How far an arm swings to push the cube over onto its side.150
Overshoot °How far the turntable goes past a layer turn before coming back. A little helps a stiff cube line up; set it to 0 to switch it off.6
Speed °/sMotor speed for every move. Start slower while trying a new build.600

The right arm is a mirror image of the left, so the program runs it the other way round, and the same angles work for both arms. If the turntable turns the wrong way, use the opposite direction in the program's table = Motor(…) line. The settings are kept in this browser, and Defaults puts them back. To find good angles, start with the defaults and a slow speed, with a hand near the centre button, and adjust a little at a time.

View plan shows the same plan as JSON, for your own programs. It contains:

  • codes: the codes above, in order;
  • steps: each step with its kind, its move in cube notation, and the quarter turns of the turntable or the arm that tips;
  • place: the colours that go on top and in front;
  • start: the cube as it sits in the robot, as 54 stickers in the order U R F D L B;
  • solution, the step counts and the estimated seconds.

Some numbers

Positions43,252,003,274,489,856,000 (about 4.3 × 10¹⁹) that can be reached by turning.
God's numberEvery position can be solved in 20 moves or fewer when a half turn counts as one move (the face-turn, or half-turn, metric), and in 26 or fewer when it counts as two (the quarter-turn metric). Proved for face turns by Rokicki, Kociemba, Davidson and Dethridge (2010), and for quarter turns by Rokicki and Davidson (2014).
The superflipEvery edge flipped in its place, everything else solved: one of the positions that need the full 20 face turns.

How it works

The model

The cube is 54 stickers in a fixed order (up, right, front, down, left, back faces, nine each). Every sticker has a position and an outward direction in space, and a move turns the stickers in its layers a quarter turn about its axis. The moves are worked out from that geometry, not typed in as tables, so the same code handles face turns, slices (M, E, S), wide turns and whole-cube rotations (x, y, z).

Scrambles from a random position

A scramble of random moves does not make every position equally likely. Cube Studio draws a position uniformly at random from all 43 quintillion, asks the solver how to solve it, and plays that solution backwards: the scramble makes exactly the drawn position. This is how competition scrambles are made.

The solver

Kociemba's two-phase algorithm: first bring the cube into a subgroup that can be finished with half turns of the front, back, left and right faces and any turns of the top and bottom, then finish it within that subgroup. It finds solutions of about 20 to 22 moves in a few tens of milliseconds. They are short, but not always the shortest possible. The implementation is cubejs (MIT), run in a background worker so the page never freezes.

Checked twice

The tests check the model on its own:

  • every move four times is no move;
  • R U repeated 105 times comes back to the start;
  • the superflip sequence gives the superflip.

The model also agrees with the solver library on 300 random move sequences. The independent model then checks the solver: it plays 1,000 solutions of random positions, and every one must solve the cube.
