How Tierwise plans, and what it leaves out

Method. Requirements planning, tier by tier from the consumer upstream. The forecast at each consumption point is what must arrive there each month. A warehouse ships that and builds or draws its safety stock (a cover of weeks of coming demand), and the shipment has to leave it the handling time earlier. A CMO's output for month m must be made the release time earlier (quality testing and hold), grossed up for its yield; its inputs must arrive the production lead time earlier still. What a site needs is split across its suppliers by their sourcing shares, shifted earlier by each lane's transit time and converted through the bill of materials: 1 pack = 20 sachets, 1 sachet = 25 g of powder, 1 t of powder = 0.7 t of dairy base.

Time. Planning runs month by month over 156 months, so every lead time shifts the plan exactly, including fractions of a month. The view shows 36 months, then 10 years, each year built from its own 12 months. A year's average and its busiest month are both kept.

Latitude. Demand peaks in the local cold season (southern markets six months apart). Raw-milk capacity swings with the grass season, more strongly away from the equator. Transit is estimated from great-circle distance: by road within 1,500 km, by sea beyond (an override per lane can be added in the data).

Limits. This is an infinite-capacity requirement plan (lot-for-lot): it shows where and when capacity is short, but it does not re-route volume or pre-build stock to absorb a peak. Inventory is held only at warehouses, as safety stock. There is one product. Sourcing shares are fixed over the horizon. All figures in the example are illustrative.
