The file format

One row per site, with these columns (headers in any order; unknown columns are ignored):

  • id, tier (market, warehouse, packaging, dosing, product or raw), name, country, lat, lon (decimal degrees, south and west negative).
  • Consumption points: base (demand a month), growth and longGrowth (% a year in years 1–3 and after), season (± %), launch (YYYY-MM) and ramp (months).
  • Every other site: capacity (a month, in its tier's unit). Warehouses: handling (days) and cover (weeks of safety stock). CMOs: lead and release (days) and yield (%).
  • suppliers: sites of the tier directly above, as id:share, separated by |; add :days to set a lane's transit time instead of estimating it from the distance.
  • expansions as YYYY-MM:+amount and outages as YYYY-MM:months:loss%, separated by |.
  • candidates for the investment optimiser, as label:add:capex:build:qualify, separated by | (build and qualify in months); add :opex for a running cost a year.

After the sites, an optional table headed setting,value (or a Settings sheet in Excel) sets the product, startYear, startMonth (1–12), the bill of materials (packPerUnit, kgPerSachet, rawPerProduct), the seasons (demandPeak, rawPeak, rawSeasonMax), the investment optimiser's packValue, discountRate, terminalYears and currency, and the tiers' own names and units (tier.dosing.name, tier.dosing.unit…). Comma, semicolon or tab separated; with semicolons, decimal commas are read.

The file stays in your browser: nothing is uploaded.

What reaches the shelves · within capacity

The plan within capacity: no site makes or ships more than it can, so some demand may go unsupplied. Sites build stock ahead of a peak (up to the limit you set), re-source to suppliers with room, and draw safety stock in a crunch; what still cannot be made is lost. Every consumption point counts the same unless you give it a priority in the editor.

How Tierwise plans, and what it leaves out

Method. Requirements planning, tier by tier from the consumer upstream. The forecast at each consumption point is what must arrive there each month. A warehouse ships that and builds or draws its safety stock (a cover of weeks of coming demand), and the shipment has to leave it the handling time earlier. A CMO's output for month m must be made the release time earlier (quality testing and hold), grossed up for its yield; its inputs must arrive the production lead time earlier still. What a site needs is split across its suppliers by their sourcing shares, shifted earlier by each lane's transit time and converted through the bill of materials: in the example, 1 pack = 20 sachets, 1 sachet = 25 g of powder, 1 t of powder = 0.7 t of dairy base.

Tracing. The plan is linear, so every site's load splits exactly into the consumption points behind it and into its direct customers: that is what the selected site shows under "What drives the load".

Capacity to add. For each site, the additions that keep it at or under the target in every month, reviewed once a year: each is sized for the busiest month until the next review (for a raw-ingredient site, the leanest milk month counts) and rounded up to two significant digits.

Time. Planning runs month by month over 156 months, so every lead time shifts the plan exactly, including fractions of a month. The view shows 36 months, then 10 years, each year built from its own 12 months. A year's average and its busiest month are both kept.

Latitude. Demand peaks in the local cold season (southern markets six months apart). Raw-milk capacity swings with the grass season, more strongly away from the equator. Transit is estimated from great-circle distance: by road within 1,500 km, by sea beyond, unless a lane sets its own.

Limits. The requirement plan assumes unlimited capacity (lot-for-lot): it shows where and when capacity is short, and how much to add. Inventory is held only at warehouses, as safety stock. There is one product. Sourcing shares are fixed over the horizon. All figures in the example are illustrative.

Within capacity. Switched on, a linear program solves the whole 13-year horizon at once, month by month, with no site above its capacity. It decides what each site makes or ships, which lane carries it, how much stock to build ahead of a peak (stock may be held up to the months you choose; warehouses keep at least two months for safety stock) and what demand cannot be supplied. Unmet demand costs far more than anything else; then safety stock below its target, re-sourcing away from the planned shares, holding stock and transport. Sourcing can keep the planned shares, re-source among the existing (qualified) lanes, or open every lane from the tier above. The model runs over a warm-up before the first month and a run-out after the last, so the edges of the horizon are not distorted. It is solved by HiGHS in your browser, in a few seconds.
